Two weeks, the two of us, driving across Kyrgyzstan. We climbed from Bishkek to Son-Kul Lake over the Kalmak-Ashuu pass, headed deep into the Tian Shan for Köl-Suu Lake, Tash Rabat and Panda Pass, then circled Issyk-Kul before coming home through the Chuy valley. On the clock: a little over 2,200 km, plenty of dirt tracks, nights in yurts, and some of the finest hikes we have ever done.

<Before>

We had booked everything before leaving: the accommodation on Booking, the car through avtoprokat.kg.

A pleasant surprise once there: driving in Kyrgyzstan is fairly easy. People are calm, the rules of the road are respected, and the main roads are brand-new highways, often four lanes wide, built by China as part of the new Silk Road.

<Tip kind="warning">
Some areas, Köl-Suu Lake among them, require a border permit: about €20 per person, to be requested in advance. The easiest way is to let the Köl-Suu camp handle it: you send them your passport, then pick up the permit in Naryn, in a souvenir shop, where you pay on the spot. Five minutes, no more.
</Tip>

<Tip kind="warning">
Never drive at night, for safety. And keep to the limits: there are speed cameras absolutely everywhere.
</Tip>

<Tip kind="tip">
For navigation, Yandex Maps is your best friend: it shows speed cameras, roadworks and closed roads. Maps.me complements it well for points of interest off the network.
</Tip>

</Before>

A flight to Istanbul, a connection, and there we were at Manas, the airport north of Bishkek, around 4 in the morning. Immigration takes a good hour. The hotel had arranged a transfer for about fifteen euros, which saved us haggling with taxi drivers at that hour.

<Tip kind="tip">
Even if you land at 6 a.m., book the hotel night: being able to sleep a few hours before the trip begins makes all the difference.

<Region id="son-koul">

<Day n={3} title="Bishkek → Son-Kul" />

At 8:30 the car was waiting outside the hotel: the agency had delivered it to the door, and ten minutes later we were on the road. A quick stop at the supermarket on the way out of Bishkek to stock up for the picnics, then on to Tokmok and <Place at="kotchkor">Kochkor</Place>, which we reached just before noon.

<Tip kind="tip">
Do your shopping at the supermarket on the way out of Bishkek: it is the last easy place to stock up for three or four days.
</Tip>

In Kochkor, we had a look round the small bazaar to buy some fruit, then, around 1 p.m., pushed open the door of the town museum. Not of great interest, but so kitsch that it made us laugh a lot. We had lunch there before tackling the climb.

<Tip kind="warning" at="kotchkor">
Fill up in Kochkor: it is the last petrol station before the track.
</Tip>

<Tip kind="warning" at="col-kalmak-ashuu">
Don’t leave Kochkor after 3 p.m., and set off even a little earlier if you want some margin.
</Tip>

We set off again around 3. After twenty kilometres or so of tarmac, the road turns into a track and climbs towards the <Place at="col-kalmak-ashuu">Kalmak-Ashuu pass</Place>. It is easy on the whole, probably passable without a 4×4, but we made slow progress: the view had us stopping at every other bend. We crossed the pass just before 4.

The track along the lake is much worse than the one up to the pass, very bumpy: don’t underestimate how long it takes.
</Tip>

We reached the yurt camp just after 6, right in time for the sunset.

After dinner, we drove another five kilometres towards the hills and climbed halfway up to photograph the Milky Way.

<Tip kind="night" at="son-koul-camp">
Some fifteen yurts set in an arc facing the lake, and a large, fully glazed common room looking over the water. Dinner is not included: €8 to €10 per person for soup, a main course and dessert, in a cuisine at the crossroads of Russian, Chinese and Iranian influences, with lots of meat, even in the soup. The wi-fi runs on Starlink, as in most camps, but there is no phone signal at all by the lake. The coal stove, lit after dinner, turns the yurt into an oven in the evening; poorly insulated, it cools quickly and the morning is chilly, at over 3,000 m. With all the blankets provided, though, we were never cold.
</Tip>

We went through the small village of <Place at="ak-tal">Ak Tal</Place>, then the road to Naryn got harder: it is under construction, alternating tarmac and gravel. That stretch took us about two hours, and should take half as long once it is finished. A few kilometres before Naryn, we stopped at the <Place at="panorama-sur-naryn">viewpoint over the Naryn canyon</Place>, where a somewhat neglected picnic area gave us lunch with a view.

<Tip kind="tip" at="panorama-sur-naryn">
The viewpoint is poorly signposted: look it up on Maps.me or Yandex. It is on the right of the road when coming from Ak Tal.

Naryn is a fairly big town, where we did our shopping and filled up. It is also where we picked up our <Place at="naryn-permit">border permits</Place> for Köl-Suu, in a souvenir shop: about €20 per person, and it was done in five minutes.

<Tip kind="tip" at="naryn-permit">
Naryn is the last town before Köl-Suu: make the most of it to do your shopping and fill up.

Just after 1 p.m., we headed south. Another thirty kilometres or so of tarmac, then the <Place at="kol-suu-track">Köl-Suu track</Place>, very good on the whole: you can safely drive at 50 km/h most of the time. Around 2 p.m., we met several herds coming down from the summer pastures, and got stuck several times in the middle of veritable seas of sheep.

Halfway along, a military checkpoint stopped us: permits, passports, and the barrier went up after five minutes. It takes three hours from the end of the tarmac to the lake, driving gently.

<Tip kind="warning" at="kol-suu-track">
Keep your permit and passport at hand for the military checkpoint. And beware of rain: the last 20 km before the lake can turn extremely muddy.

We reached the Köl-Suu yurt camp around 4:30, with time to wander round the “village” and along the lake before dinner.

<Tip kind="night" at="kol-suu-camp">
The place is very remote, but the area where the yurts stand is narrow: a hundred or so yurts packed fairly close, like a little village. In the big common room you eat at long tables, and we ended up getting on well with our neighbours: a really nice evening.
</Tip>

We set off for <Place at="kol-suu">Köl-Suu Lake</Place> around 8, and all the way up we didn’t meet a soul: the feeling of being alone in the world. The hike is fairly easy. At first you follow the river; the night’s rain had left a few muddy stretches, and in places you have to use your hands on the rocks along a cliff. Nothing like climbing: at worst, you end up with your feet in the cold water.

<Plates>
  <Photo id="KGZ-37" />
  <Photo id="KGZ-38" />
</Plates>

The end of the climb is only 200 m of ascent, but at over 3,500 m you feel it. And then, around 10, the lake appears: turquoise blue, at the bottom of a very deep valley, between two walls of rock.

Having arrived first, we had it to ourselves for twenty minutes or so, before the other hikers turned up one by one. And as if that weren’t enough, the clouds cleared at that very moment, and the light on the water was superb.

You can take a boat out on the lake. We didn’t, for lack of time, and looking back we regret it: it would have been worth an extra hour or two. On the way back, the sun had dried the mud, and the path was much easier.

<Tip kind="tip" at="kol-suu-camp">
Negotiate an early breakfast, around 7:30, to set off first: we had the lake to ourselves for twenty minutes or so. And keep an hour or two for the boat trip.
</Tip>

We were back at the camp around noon. A quick picnic, then the same track again: three hours to get back to the tarmac. A precautionary fill-up in At-Bashy, then another hour to Tash Rabat. The road out of At-Bashy carries a constant stream of lorries between China and Kyrgyzstan, and the one up to Tash Rabat was being surfaced: half finished, half under works.

<Tip kind="warning">
Between At-Bashy and Tash Rabat, the lorries running between China and Kyrgyzstan never stop; they are only banned between 6 p.m. and 8 a.m.
</Tip>

Around 5 p.m., we drove up to the head of the valley to see the <Place at="tash-rabat">caravanserai</Place>. No luck: it was closed for consolidation works, for about a year, so we had to make do with the outside. When it is open, allow half an hour to an hour: it is not very big, and there is not much to see.

<Tip kind="warning" at="tash-rabat">
The caravanserai has been closed for consolidation works since September 2026, for about a year: only the outside can be seen.
</Tip>

<Day n={7} title="Tash Rabat and Panda Pass" />

<Pair photo="KGZ-42">

<Tip kind="hike" at="tash-rabat" name="Panda Pass, the view over Chatyr-Kul Lake" distance={22} ascent={1000} difficulty="hard" course="out-and-back">
Start from a tiny car park, 4 or 5 km past the caravanserai. Follow a small stream with several fords, turn right at the end of the valley, then comes the final climb up a scree slope: a false pass, and behind it a second, even bigger scree slope. The last 200 m of ascent are very hard. From the pass, the view opens onto the plain and, in the distance, Chatyr-Kul Lake. Allow the whole day.
</Tip>

<Tip kind="tip" at="panda-pass">
Drive 4 or 5 km past the caravanserai, to the small car park at the foot of the scree: you will skip the least interesting part of the hike.
</Tip>

A big day. After breakfast, we drove back to the caravanserai, then another 4 or 5 km to a tiny car park, three or four spaces at the foot of the scree. On the menu: 22 km and 1,000 m of ascent. We started by following a small stream, crossing it several times, hopping from stone to stone.

At the end of the valley, you turn right, and the real climb begins.

</Pair>

<Photo id="KGZ-43" />

We would have the day almost to ourselves: only two other people met. The final climb is up a scree slope, and the GPX track we had downloaded turned out to be inaccurate. Then came the false pass: once on the ridge, we discovered a second scree slope behind it, even bigger than the first. Very hard on morale. The last 200 m of ascent are intense: we stopped talking, and it all came down to willpower.

<Tip kind="warning" at="panda-pass">
Beware of the false pass: behind the first ridge hides a second, bigger scree slope, and an accurate GPX track helps a lot. The weather also changes very fast up there: wind and snow can arrive within minutes.
</Tip>

Around 12:30, we finally reached the pass. The view is superb, and it was worth the effort.

<Region id="issyk-koul">

<Day n={8} title="Tash Rabat → Bokonbaevo" />

We left Tash Rabat in the morning, heading for At-Bashy. Just before 9, we made a short stop at the <Place at="kochoy-korgon-2">Kochoy Korgon</Place> fortress by the road: ten minutes are enough to see the ruins of its walls. There is supposedly a museum next door, but according to every review it never opens.

Next we had to go back up to <Place at="naryn">Naryn</Place>, then straight on to <Place at="kotchkor-ville">Kochkor</Place>, the village of <Place at="ottuk">Ottuk</Place> and finally <Place at="bokonbaevo">Bokonbaevo</Place>. Between Naryn and Kochkor the road is good; between Kochkor and Ottuk it is brand new, four lanes and almost deserted. Things got worse between Ottuk and Bokonbaevo, where new sections alternate with slow-moving roadworks: the drive will be much quicker once they are finished. We reached Bokonbaevo around 1:30, just in time to fill up and have lunch.

Around 3:30, we covered the 20 km to the <Place at="skazka">Skazka canyon</Place>, the “Fairy Tale Canyon”, the last two on an easy track. It was the first place on the trip with real crowds of tourists. Several trails start from the entrance; we began by climbing to the viewpoint on the ridge behind the canyon, the most beautiful of all. In the late afternoon, with the sun behind you, the red rocks stand out magnificently.

Then we happily got lost in the canyon’s gullies.

<Tip kind="warning" at="skazka">
The gullies all look alike and it is easy to get lost. Bring good shoes: it is slippery.
</Tip>

<Tip kind="night" at="ton">
A camp of glass domes, real geodesic domes facing the lake, near Bokonbaevo. It is luxurious, but half an hour’s drive from the canyon: better to sleep on the Barskoon side and save the detour.
</Tip>

<Day n={13} title="Tokmok → Kegeti" />

Just before 9, we went looking for the Tokmok livestock market, which we had seen mentioned in several guidebooks. Impossible to find: even the locals didn’t know what we were talking about. It has probably disappeared.

<Tip kind="tip" at="karakol">

<Day n={14} title="Kegeti → Bishkek" />

To leave Kegeti, we chose to follow the mountains on the south side of the valley rather than take the main road again. Around 10, we passed near Issyk-Ata and its sanatorium; there would be fine hikes to do around there, but we were short of time.

<Photo id="KGZ-87" />

We wanted to visit the <Place at="ata-beyit">Ata-Beyit memorial</Place>, but it was closed that day.

Around noon, we arrived at <Place at="ala-archa">Ala-Archa national park</Place>. You have to leave the car in the big car park at the entrance, and there is an entry fee. A shuttle leaves every fifteen minutes to take visitors up the valley to the trailheads, a 15- to 20-minute ride, and drops them at a small ski resort with its restaurants and shops. We were aiming for the Ak-Sai waterfall, but tiredness got the better of our motivation.

<Tip kind="tip" at="ala-archa">
